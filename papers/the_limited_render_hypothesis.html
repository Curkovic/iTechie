The Limited-Render Hypothesis

What if the universe only renders what someone can reach? Cosmic horizons, the speed of light and the holographic bound read like the design choices of a resource-limited simulation. A hypothesis, one possible test, the arguments against it, and a reminder that looking like science is not the same as being proven.

Abstract

We propose that a simulated universe could save computation through cosmological inaccessibility: only regions that conscious observers can causally reach are rendered in full detail, while everything beyond exists as rules, not data. Vast distances, a maximum speed for information and an expanding universe would then not be accidents of nature but design features: they make the universe feel infinite while keeping it cheap to run. We map the idea onto real physics (cosmic event horizons, the speed of light, the holographic bound), point to one published test that could support or weaken it, and list the strongest arguments against it.

01 // Introduction

Bostrom (2003) argued that if civilisations can run detailed simulations of minds, simulated observers could vastly outnumber real ones. Most follow-up ideas focus on render-on-observation: detail appears only when something is measured, an intuition often borrowed from quantum mechanics (Carroll, 2019). This paper asks a different question. Not “how is detail produced when we look?” but “how does a simulation make sure we can never look too far?” Our answer: by making most of the universe permanently out of reach, and letting the observers conclude it is simply infinite.

02 // The hypothesis

A resource-limited simulated universe would follow four principles:

  1. Only regions that observers can causally reach are fully instantiated.
  2. Everything beyond exists as consistent rules, generated only when needed.
  3. Physical constants and cosmic scales are tuned so that most of the universe can never be reached.
  4. Observers, unable to find an edge, develop theories of infinity.

03 // What real physics already looks like

A horizon that grows. Because the expansion of the universe is accelerating, light emitted today from beyond roughly 16 billion light-years will never reach us, and that boundary closes in on more galaxies over time. Krauss and Scherrer (2007) showed that in the far future, observers will see only their own merged galaxy and nothing else. The universe is not just big; it is actively making itself unreachable.

A speed limit for information. Nothing, not even a signal, travels faster than light. For a simulation this behaves like a loading distance: no observer can demand detail from far away faster than the system can deliver it.

Storage that scales with surface, not volume. The maximum information a region of space can contain is set by the area of its boundary, not by its volume (Bekenstein, 1981; ’t Hooft, 1993; Susskind, 1995). To an engineer this looks like compression: a three-dimensional world that may be cheaper to encode than it appears.

Level of detail. Distant galaxies reach us only as light: a few photons, never matter we can sample. Video games use the same trick, giving far objects low-resolution models and full detail only up close (Smelik et al., 2014).

A quiet universe. Civilisations separated by enormous distances rarely or never interact, and interacting minds are the most expensive thing to simulate. Seen this way, the silence behind the Fermi paradox is the cheapest possible setting.

04 // How it could be tested

Beane, Davoudi and Savage (2014) proposed that if spacetime were computed on a grid, the highest-energy cosmic rays would show a faint directional pattern aligned with that grid. It is a concrete, measurable signal. Finding it would not prove a simulation, but it would show that space has a structure no ordinary theory predicts. Not finding it would push any such grid below what we can detect, which weakens the idea without killing it.

Other directions: looking for hard limits on information density at cosmic scales, and checking whether physical constants are as finely tuned toward inaccessibility as the hypothesis needs.

05 // The case against

  • Consistency is expensive. Point the Hubble telescope at an “empty” patch of sky and thousands of galaxies appear, each with a history that agrees with everything else we know. A simulation would have to keep the past consistent everywhere someone might look, which may cost more than it saves.
  • Ordinary physics explains it too. Horizons, the speed of light and the holographic bound all follow from general relativity and quantum theory with no simulator needed. Occam’s razor prefers the simpler explanation.
  • It can always escape. Any failed test can be answered with “the simulation is better than that.” A hypothesis that survives every possible result is not making a scientific claim.

06 // Conclusion

The Limited-Render Hypothesis reads cosmic horizons, the speed of light and the holographic bound as signs of a universe built to look infinite while staying cheap to run. It fits the evidence. So does the ordinary explanation. Until a test like the one above finds something only a simulation can explain, it remains what it is: an idea.

Author's note

The idea is mine; it grew out of hours of discussion and I still like it. But this page has an abstract, numbered sections, a test and a reference list, and none of that makes it true. Anyone can make an idea look like a scientific paper, and the internet is full of them. Something looking like science doesn’t make it real. Nothing is as it is until it’s proven.
