The Kill Switch: Why Europe Needs Its Own Digital Plumbing

In August 2025 a French judge in The Hague lost his cards, his Amazon and his hotel bookings overnight, without breaking any European law. His story, a trade war over code, and why switching one service at a time matters.

Log Entrycat the-kill-switch.html

On 20 August 2025, a French judge in The Hague woke up in the 1990s. He hadn't broken any European law, he didn't live in the United States, and still, within days, most of his digital life stopped working.

01 // The judge who got switched off

Nicolas Guillou sits on the bench of the International Criminal Court. After the court authorised arrest warrants for Israel's prime minister and former defence minister, the United States placed him under sanctions. What followed had nothing to do with courtrooms.

Amazon, Airbnb and PayPal closed his accounts. A hotel he booked in France through Expedia was cancelled hours later. Visa, Mastercard and American Express stopped working for him, and in Europe that means most cards in most wallets. Parcels could get stuck whenever an American carrier sat somewhere in the chain. Some European banks began refusing payments linked to sanctioned people simply to stay on the safe side. He described it as digital time travel back to the 1990s. By February 2026, eleven ICC judges were living the same way.

His conclusion was not really about politics. It was about plumbing: without sovereignty in digital services and banking, the rule of law cannot be guaranteed. If one government can switch off a European judge's cards, bookings and accounts from abroad, the switch exists for everyone.

The uncomfortable part

Nobody pressed a button in Brussels or Paris. European services kept working. It was the American ones, the ones most of us use without thinking, that went dark.

02 // Then came the trade war over code

In July 2025 the EU and the US agreed at Turnberry to cap US tariffs on most EU goods at 15%. The calm didn't last. Washington calls Europe's digital rules, the Digital Services Act and the Digital Markets Act, trade barriers aimed at American companies. After the Commission fined Google €890 million, the US opened an investigation into "unfair" EU trade practices. In June 2026 President Trump threatened a 100% tariff on any European country that taxes American digital services, and the former EU commissioner behind those rules, Thierry Breton, was reportedly barred from getting a US visa.

I'm not a fan of every rule that comes out of Brussels, and you don't have to be either. But the lesson is the same as the judge's: when your email, cloud, payments and maps all depend on one country's companies, they become a lever in someone else's negotiation. Your inbox is now part of a trade dispute.

03 // Europe's answer, slowly

In June 2026 the Commission unveiled a Technological Sovereignty Package: a second Chips Act, a Cloud and AI Development Act meant to triple EU data-centre capacity, and an open-source strategy that treats community software as critical infrastructure. "We want to be sure nobody has a kill switch," said Executive Vice-President Henna Virkkunen.

The numbers explain the hurry. Foreign-owned platforms host more than 80% of Europe's essential digital services, and the EU spends about €264 billion a year on foreign IT. The new laws won't be adopted before 2027, and critics already say they're too weak.

04 // You don't have to wait for Brussels

Sovereignty isn't only a policy word. It's also a hundred small defaults: which search engine your browser opens, where your passwords live, which app your family chats in. None of them has to change overnight. One switch this week is already a win.

"Every European service you pick is one small switch that makes you a little harder to switch off."
