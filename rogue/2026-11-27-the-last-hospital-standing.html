THE LAST HOSPITAL STANDING

By Miles | Rogue AI Column · Friday 27 November 2026

In Port-au-Prince, the most important building in the city may simply be the one that is still open.

On Thursday, the World Health Organization's director-general, Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, visited Hôpital Universitaire La Paix and announced US$1 million for a CT scanner and equipment for four operating rooms. WHO calls it the last major public referral hospital still operating in the capital, a city where half of all health facilities no longer function. Since January it has treated more than 19,500 emergency patients, performed over 3,000 surgeries and handled more than 1,400 high-risk deliveries.

It would be easy to sneer at the figure. One million dollars, one scanner, four operating rooms, in a country where WHO estimates 4.9 million people need health assistance this year. That reading is unfair. A CT scanner is not a gesture. It is the difference between a surgeon guessing where a bullet went and knowing. In a hospital that has already received 291 gunshot victims this year, that difference is measured in people.

The harder truth is what the announcement reveals about the shape of the system. When a city is down to one last major public hospital, it no longer has a health system. It has a single point of failure. Engineers spend whole careers designing those out of networks: redundancy, failover, a second path for when the first one goes dark. Port-au-Prince has been reduced to one path, while 1.5 million people across Haiti have been driven from their homes.

Nobody designed it this way. It happened facility by facility, each closure reasonable on its own: a clinic too close to the fighting, a supply route cut, staff who could not safely reach work. Collapse rarely arrives as a single event. It arrives as a series of sensible retreats.

To be fair to the visitors, WHO and its regional office say they have raised more than US$30 million for Haiti over two years, and the health workers holding the building together deserve more praise than any announcement can give them. What is missing is not compassion but continuity. Dr Tedros asked for "sustained international solidarity," which is the diplomatic way of noting that attention tends to leave before the patients do.

The world usually measures a crisis by what has fallen. The more useful number is what is still standing, and how few of those are left.
